\begin{abstract}
Large Language Model serving is bottlenecked by memory capacity for Key-Value (KV) caches. Multi-turn conversations force frequent cache eviction and costly recomputation (the ``cold-start'' problem). We build a three-tier KV cache persistence system (VRAM $\to$ NVMe $\to$ object storage) and evaluate six eviction policies (LRU, a recency heuristic, learned binary classification, static value-density with and without admission control, and a lifetime-normalized space-time density policy) under a protocol built to avoid common evaluation artifacts: virtual-clock trace replay, persona-driven synthetic workloads, tier-discounted value accounting, and paired comparisons across 10 seeds with 95\% confidence intervals.

Our hypothesis was that a learned policy would beat LRU on cache hit rate. It does not: LRU wins hit rate on every constrained workload we test, by 11 points over a logistic classifier on enterprise traffic and by 12 on power-user traffic, with all paired deltas significant. The interesting result is what that verdict conceals. On the same enterprise workload, LRU delivers the \emph{least} GPU time saved of any policy in our study, and a value-density policy that concedes 21 hit-rate points saves 1.99$\times$ as much (paired 95\% CI $[+30.4, +42.4]$ GPU-s/day). Ranking by hit rate selects the worst available policy while reporting it as the best.

We explain when and why the metrics diverge. A hit is worth nothing unless the prefill it avoids exceeds the restore it costs, a threshold set by the break-even context length $N^{*}$, and above that threshold savings grow superlinearly in context length. Hit rate encodes neither effect. Consequently the metrics agree when cached items are near-uniform in value, on our power-user workload, whose sessions sit far above $N^{*}$, and under Llama-2-70B economics, where grouped-query attention places $N^{*}$ at 12 tokens so that essentially every hit pays, and diverge sharply when the workload straddles $N^{*}$. We derive $N^{*}$ analytically, show it varies four orders of magnitude across architectures, and validate the model against measured hardware.

A ground-truth ablation isolates the effect from every confound: two clairvoyant policies that both cache \emph{perfectly}, 100.0\% hit rate against 100.0\%, a paired difference of zero on every seed, still deliver significantly different value, because once nothing need be evicted the remaining decision is which tier holds each entry, and hit rate cannot see it.

We also report two evaluation bugs of our own, because they moved the verdict in opposite directions and neither perturbed rankings at random. A wall-clock replay bug flattered the learned policy; a cost model containing no architecture at all, pricing prefill with constants whose linear/quadratic crossover sat an order of magnitude off any real transformer, flattered LRU, and invalidated a ``static value density collapses'' finding we had previously reported. Simple recency policies are insensitive to broken time, broken features, and broken value models alike; every richer policy is exposed to all three. Harness bugs therefore flatter whichever side of a comparison depends on the broken component, which we suggest is a systematic hazard for the learned-caching literature rather than an accident of our pipeline.

End-to-end validation with TinyLlama-1.1B measures a 19.1$\times$ TTFT gain on CPU, a T4 context-length sweep that crosses parity between 440 and 1,792 tokens, bracketing the predicted crossover, and an in-memory-control ablation in which the storage round-trip adds no output divergence beyond what any cache-resume path already incurs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Serving a large language model proceeds in two phases. A \emph{prefill} phase processes the entire prompt in parallel, populating the KV cache: the attention keys and values of every token at every layer. A \emph{decode} phase then generates one token at a time against that cache. Prefill cost grows quadratically with context length, while the cache it produces grows only linearly (\S2). In a multi-turn conversation, the cache is what makes turn boundaries cheap: as long as it survives in VRAM, a follow-up turn pays only for its new tokens.

VRAM has no room for idle sessions, however. A single Llama-2-7B session at its full 4k context holds 2.1\,GB of KV state (\S2.1), so an 80\,GB A100 fits only ${\sim}30$ such sessions after weights, while a production service handles thousands of users whose think time between turns ranges from seconds to days. Serving engines such as vLLM therefore evict idle caches~\cite{kwon2023pagedattention}, and when the user returns, the entire conversation history must be re-prefilled from scratch. This is the \emph{cold-start problem}: re-prefill wastes quadratically growing GPU compute and inflates time-to-first-token (TTFT) from tens of milliseconds to multiple seconds at long contexts~\cite{liu2024cachegen}.

Tiered persistence is the natural fix: demote idle caches down a storage hierarchy (VRAM $\to$ NVMe $\to$ object storage) and restore them on return, paying $O(N)$ bytes moved instead of $O(N^2)$ FLOPs recomputed. Production systems have begun doing exactly this~\cite{gao2024cachedattention,qin2025mooncake,zhong2024lmcache}. But persistence does not eliminate the eviction problem; it relocates it. Every tier is still finite, so some policy must decide which sessions each tier retains and which fall out of the hierarchy entirely.

That policy decision looks like fertile ground for learning. Sessions are far from interchangeable: they differ by orders of magnitude in size, their recompute cost grows quadratically where their footprint grows linearly, and whether a user will return appears predictable from history (time of day, past return behavior, conversation length). LRU sees none of this. We therefore hypothesized that a learned policy, which estimates each session's probability of resumption and evicts the least promising, would beat LRU on cache hit rate.

\emph{The data falsified this hypothesis}, and diagnosing why became the contribution. LRU wins hit rate on every constrained workload, and no amount of feature engineering closed the gap. But the diagnosis did not end there, because the metric turned out to be the problem. Measured by GPU time actually saved, the same LRU that wins every hit-rate comparison delivers the \emph{least} value of any policy we tested on enterprise traffic, and policies that lose to it by 11--21 hit-rate points save up to twice as much compute (\S\ref{sec:decouple}).

The reason is that hit rate assumes every hit is worth the same, and in KV caching they are not, in two distinct ways. A hit is worth \emph{nothing} unless the prefill it avoids exceeds the restore it costs, a threshold given by the break-even context length $N^{*}$ (\S\ref{sec:breakeven}); and above that threshold, savings grow superlinearly with context length, so a few large sessions can outweigh many small ones. A policy optimizing hit rate maximizes the count of hits while ignoring both effects, which is why it can win that metric and lose the one that pays for hardware.

A ground-truth ablation bounds the diagnosis: replaying the same traces with policies that read the future separates ``the objective is wrong'' from ``the predictor is weak,'' and shows that even clairvoyant policies with identical, perfect hit rates differ measurably in value (\S\ref{sec:oracle}).

Finally, we report a methodological result. Reaching the conclusions above required correcting our own evaluation twice, and the two corrections moved the verdict in opposite directions: a wall-clock replay bug had flattered the learned policy, and a cost model with no architecture in it had flattered LRU, at one point supporting a ``static value density collapses'' finding that did not survive (\S\ref{sec:lru_strong}). The asymmetry is structural rather than accidental. LRU consumes one input; every richer policy consumes more, and is exposed to defects in each. Harness bugs therefore flatter whichever side of a comparison depends on the broken component, which makes baseline-versus-learned comparisons sensitive to evaluation fidelity.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A three-tier KV cache persistence system with pluggable eviction backends (LRU, heuristic, learned classification, value-density, space-time density) and an evaluation protocol designed to remove common artifacts: virtual-clock replay, persona-driven workloads, replay of the Azure LLM inference production trace, tier-discounted value accounting, and paired multi-seed statistics.
  \item Evidence that LRU is a stronger hit-rate baseline than the learned-caching literature assumes: it wins that metric on every constrained workload we test, with all paired deltas significant at 95\%.
  \item The demonstration that hit rate and delivered value \emph{reverse} on realistic workloads: the hit-rate winner is the value loser, by up to 1.99$\times$, so hit rate alone is not merely imprecise but actively misleading as a target for KV-cache eviction research.
  \item A mechanism that predicts when the two metrics agree, in terms of the break-even length $N^{*}$ and the superlinearity of prefill, confirmed across two model architectures and three workloads.
  \item An analytic break-even model for when restoration beats recomputation, in which $N^{*}$ varies by four orders of magnitude with model architecture, validated against measured GPU and CPU prefill latencies.
  \item Two self-reported evaluation defects, moving the verdict in opposite directions, and the observation that harness bugs flatter whichever side of a comparison depends on the broken component rather than perturbing rankings at random.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}

\subsection{Self-Attention and the KV Cache}
Each transformer layer projects its input into query, key, and value matrices and computes scaled dot-product attention over all positions~\cite{vaswani2017attention}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V.
  \label{eq:attention}
\end{equation}
During autoregressive decoding every new token attends to the keys and values of all tokens before it, so inference engines cache the per-layer $K$ and $V$ tensors rather than recompute them. Prefilling an $N$-token prompt costs $O(N^2)$ attention FLOPs, while the resulting cache grows linearly:
\begin{equation}
  S(N) = 2 \cdot L \cdot H_{kv} \cdot d \cdot b \cdot N,
  \label{eq:kvsize}
\end{equation}
for $L$ layers, $H_{kv}$ KV heads, head dimension $d$, and $b$ bytes per element. Instantiated for the models in this paper (FP16): TinyLlama-1.1B ($L{=}22$, $H_{kv}{=}4$, $d{=}64$) holds 22\,KB per token; Llama-2-7B ($32, 32, 128$) holds 512\,KB per token, or 2.1\,GB for a single session at its full 4k context; Llama-2-70B's grouped-query attention ($80, 8, 128$) holds 320\,KB per token despite $10\times$ the parameters. The footprint is material: when serving a 13B model on a 40\,GB A100, roughly 65\% of memory holds weights and over 30\% holds KV cache~\cite{kwon2023pagedattention}. VRAM is scarce, so eviction is unavoidable when multiple sessions compete.

\subsection{Inference Is Memory-Bound}
Compute has outpaced memory on modern accelerators: an A100 delivers 312 TFLOP/s of FP16 compute against 1.5--2.0\,TB/s of HBM bandwidth, and both attention and the decode phase as a whole are bottlenecked by memory traffic rather than FLOPs~\cite{dao2022flashattention,kwon2023pagedattention}. FlashAttention obtains its speedups purely by reducing bytes moved between HBM and on-chip SRAM, without changing the computation. Tiered KV persistence applies the same IO-centric accounting one level further down the hierarchy: when the KV bytes of idle sessions contend for a fixed-size fast tier, the governing questions are which bytes are worth their residency and what it costs to move them back, and both are questions about data movement, not compute.

\subsection{B\'{e}l\'{a}dy's Algorithm}
The provably optimal offline eviction policy~\cite{belady1966replacement} evicts the item whose next access occurs furthest in the future: $\arg\max_{i} \text{next\_access}(i)$. For uniform-size, uniform-cost items, LRU is an effective online proxy by assuming temporal locality.

\subsection{Weighted Caching}
When items have varying sizes and costs, the uniform assumption fails. The problem maps to a dynamic Fractional Knapsack. As formalized by Young~\cite{young1994caching} and made practical by GreedyDual-Size~\cite{cao1997greedydualsize}, cost-aware eviction must account for value density per byte. LHD~\cite{beckmann2018lhd} further showed that for \emph{dynamic} caches the correct denominator is not size alone but size $\times$ expected lifetime, which they call ``hit density.'' In KV caching, ``value'' is the quadratic GPU compute saved by avoiding a cold-start prefill, and ``weight'' is the linear VRAM consumed; our results (\S\ref{sec:eval}) empirically rediscover why the static form of this objective fails and the lifetime-normalized form is required.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{LLM serving systems.} PagedAttention~\cite{kwon2023pagedattention} partitions the KV cache into fixed-size blocks, eliminating fragmentation and enabling memory sharing. vLLM manages VRAM dynamically but relies on reactive LRU eviction. CachedAttention~\cite{gao2024cachedattention} persists KV caches across multi-turn conversation rounds through a hierarchical cache, though its focus is architectural integration rather than learned eviction policies.

\textbf{Attention state reuse.} Prompt Cache~\cite{gim2024promptcache} precomputes attention states for schema-defined prompt segments and splices them into new prompts, cutting TTFT by up to 8$\times$ on GPU and 60$\times$ on CPU. LMCache~\cite{zhong2024lmcache} and CacheBlend~\cite{yao2024cacheblend} focus on cross-request prefix sharing: caching system prompts shared across users. All three reuse state across prompts that share text; our work targets single-session persistence, saving user-specific conversational state over long time horizons, where the challenge is predicting which sessions will return.

\textbf{Tiering and offloading.} Mooncake~\cite{qin2025mooncake} proposes a disaggregated KV cache architecture using a tiered hierarchy across nodes. FlexGen~\cite{sheng2023flexgen} offloads weights, activations, and KV cache across GPU, CPU, and disk to maximize batch throughput on a single GPU, but its offload schedule is solved once for a static batch. Recent work persists per-agent quantized KV caches to disk for multi-agent inference on memory-constrained edge devices~\cite{shkolnikov2026agentmemory}, reporting up to 136$\times$ TTFT reductions on restore. These systems engineer the persistence and placement mechanism; we target the complementary policy layer. Where they ask ``how do we move and store cache state?'', we ask ``which entries should we keep when capacity is constrained, and by what metric should that choice be judged?''

\textbf{Token-level KV compression.} H2O~\cite{zhang2023h2o} and Scissorhands~\cite{liu2023scissorhands} exploit the observation that a small set of pivotal tokens dominates attention scores to drop most of a sequence's KV entries (up to 5$\times$ reduction), and InfiniGen~\cite{lee2024infinigen} offloads token-level state dynamically. CacheGen~\cite{liu2024cachegen} instead compresses whole KV caches into quantized bitstreams for transmission, shrinking them 3.5--4.3$\times$ with negligible quality loss. Both lines are orthogonal to our granularity: we decide which \emph{sessions} to retain across tiers, and token-level compression or KV-specific codecs could be composed with our policies to shrink what we store.

\textbf{Learned cache eviction.} LRB~\cite{song2020lrb} learns to approximate B\'{e}l\'{a}dy's oracle for CDN caching; LHD~\cite{beckmann2018lhd} maximizes hits per unit of space-time occupied. Our work differs in the value model: CDN objects have roughly uniform miss costs, whereas KV cache entries have \emph{quadratically} varying recompute costs, which changes both the correct objective (\S\ref{sec:std}) and, as we show, the correct evaluation metric.

\section{System Design}

Our architecture introduces a \texttt{TieredCacheManager} orchestrating three storage tiers:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Hot Tier (VRAM):} In-memory storage. ${\sim}0.1$ms latency, 50MB capacity.
  \item \textbf{Warm Tier (NVMe SSD):} Filesystem-backed with memory-mapped reads for files $>$10MB. ${\sim}10$ms latency, 150MB capacity.
  \item \textbf{Cold Tier (S3/MinIO):} Object storage with Zstandard compression. ${\sim}100$ms latency, 300MB capacity.
\end{enumerate}

The tier parameters mirror the hardware each stands in for: the hot tier models VRAM residency (sub-millisecond access), the warm tier a local NVMe device (${\sim}$10ms, 2\,GB/s), and the cold tier compressed object storage (${\sim}$100ms, 500\,MB/s). Capacities are scaled to the downscaled KV geometry of \S\ref{sec:setup} so that the session-count-to-capacity ratio matches a production deployment.

\textbf{Save path.} \texttt{save} serializes a session's KV tensors and writes them to the hot tier. If the hot tier is full, the manager makes space by \emph{cascading demotion}: the eviction policy selects a victim among hot entries, and that victim is demoted to the warm tier rather than deleted; if the warm tier is full, it recursively demotes warm victims to cold. Only a victim selected in the cold tier leaves the system. Eviction is therefore graceful by construction. A policy mistake at the hot or warm boundary costs a returning session only added restore latency; a mistake at the cold boundary destroys the session and forces a full re-prefill. This asymmetry is why victim selection, not tier engineering, is where policy quality matters.

\textbf{Load path.} \texttt{load} consults an in-memory index and reads the entry from whichever tier holds it. A hit is recorded against that tier, and the value accounting of \S\ref{sec:setup} discounts it by that tier's restore cost, so a cold-tier hit is worth less than a hot-tier one. On a warm or cold hit the entry is promoted back to the hot tier. A miss means the session either was never cached or fell out of the cold tier, and the serving layer must re-prefill the full history. \texttt{demote} and \texttt{promote} are also exposed directly for explicit migration.

\section{Policy V1: Eviction as Classification}
\label{sec:v1}

Our initial approach formulated eviction as supervised ML: predict $P(\text{resume})$ that a session will be accessed again within 1~hour. We engineered 9~features spanning the session's own state (age, token count, revisit count, minutes since last access), the clock (hour of day, day of week, a business-hours indicator), and the user's history (historical return rate, mean session length), and trained Logistic Regression and Gradient Boosted Trees on 7-day persona-driven traces (80/20 split, standardized features, class-balanced loss; we select by ROC-AUC rather than accuracy, which label imbalance inflates). Test AUC is 0.70 (logistic) and 0.73 (GBT); we use the logistic model in the main study for interpretability. Its strongest positive coefficient is the user's historical return rate ($+0.75$: one standard deviation multiplies the odds of resumption by $e^{0.75}{\approx}2.1\times$), which confirms the personas carry learnable signal. Candidate sets are scored in a single batched prediction per eviction sweep.

\textbf{Negative Result:} Under enterprise load, Logistic~V1 achieves a 67.9\% hit rate [66.8, 69.0] versus LRU's 79.1\% [75.9, 82.4]. The hypothesis is falsified: an 11.2-point deficit, reproduced on every workload, that no amount of feature engineering recovered. We took this at face value for most of the study. Only after correcting the value model (\S\ref{sec:lru_strong}) did we find that the same policy saves 1.73$\times$ more GPU time than the baseline it loses to (Table~\ref{tab:results}), which is the observation the rest of this paper is about.

\section{Failure Analysis: Eviction Is a Packing Problem}
\label{sec:failure}

This exposed a flaw in the formulation itself: eviction under capacity constraints is not a classification problem; it is a knapsack variant. LRU accidentally approximated correct behavior by rotating entries uniformly. Our ML models confidently kept large ``high probability'' sessions that choked capacity, forcing out dozens of smaller entries.

The arithmetic of the failure is worth stating concretely. Under the enterprise workload, an 8k-token session occupies the space of sixteen 512-token sessions. V1 retains it whenever its resumption probability exceeds each smaller entry's individually, say 0.9 against 0.6. But the sixteen displaced sessions carried $16 \times 0.6 = 9.6$ expected future hits against the large entry's 0.9. The classifier answers the question ``will this session return?'', while the capacity constraint asks a different one: ``what is the most valuable \emph{set} of sessions that fits?'' The binary criterion $\min P(\text{resume})$ ignores both entry size and variable recompute cost. The oracle ablation of \S\ref{sec:oracle} later sharpens this diagnosis: with ground-truth $P(\text{resume})$, this same objective achieves a perfect cache at our capacity, so the formulation is not intrinsically broken. Its flaw is how it behaves under prediction error: a confident false positive on one large session forces out dozens of smaller entries, so the objective amplifies the predictor's mistakes in proportion to entry size. The reformulations that follow change what is being maximized so that errors cost less.

\section{Policies V2 and V3: Eviction as Packing}

We reformulated the objective based on weighted caching theory:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Value Density} = \frac{P(\text{resume}) \times \text{RecomputeCost}(N)}{\text{SizeBytes}(N)}
  \label{eq:vd}
\end{equation}

Because prefill scales $O(N^2)$ but memory scales $O(N)$, value density grows linearly with context length: doubling a session's tokens doubles the recompute saved per byte retained. Under this objective the policy should, at any instant, pack the cache with the highest-density entries, which is precisely the greedy solution to the fractional knapsack. We also implemented an \textbf{Admission Control Gate}: sessions below a value-density threshold are denied entry to the warm tier, on the theory that filtering low-value entries at the door preserves capacity for high-value ones.

\subsection{Space-Time Density: A Cache Is a Dynamic System}
\label{sec:std}

Our evaluation revealed that static value-density collapses in practice (\S\ref{sec:eval}). A cache is not a static knapsack; it is a dynamic system governed by Little's Law. Each entry occupies \emph{space over time}. The correct objective must account for expected sojourn time $\mathbb{E}[\Delta t]$:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Space-Time Density} = \frac{P(\text{resume}) \times \text{RecomputeCost}(N)}{\text{Size}(N) \times \mathbb{E}[\Delta t]}
  \label{eq:std_eq}
\end{equation}

Under Little's Law, $L = \lambda \times W$, where $L$ is the steady-state number of resident entries, $\lambda$ their admission rate, and $W$ their average sojourn time; the law holds for any stationary arrival process, independent of distributional assumptions. The failure chain is mechanical. At full utilization the byte capacity is fixed, so $L$ equals capacity divided by mean entry size. A static density score never decreases as an entry idles (its numerator and denominator are both time-free), so the highest-density entries, which are the largest, are simply never evicted: their $W$ grows without bound, mean entry size rises, and $L$ collapses. Fewer resident sessions means fewer opportunities for any future request to hit. The policy wins every local comparison of value per byte while destroying the aggregate through the quantity it never measures: time.

This objective is structurally an adaptation of LHD's hit density~\cite{beckmann2018lhd} (expected hits per unit of size $\times$ lifetime) to the KV-cache setting. The adaptation is non-trivial in two respects: the numerator weights each hit by a \emph{quadratic} recompute cost rather than counting hits uniformly, and $P(\text{resume})$ comes from a learned session-level model rather than age-binned statistics.

\textbf{V3 Implementation.} We estimate $\mathbb{E}[\Delta t]$ per session as an exponential moving average ($\alpha{=}0.3$) of observed inter-access gaps, with a 2-hour prior for sessions with no observed gap. Because an entry idle \emph{longer} than its typical rhythm should expect a longer remaining wait, not a shorter one (the inspection paradox), we score with $\max(\text{EMA gap}, \text{current idle time})$. Candidate sets are re-scored on each eviction sweep.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Policies under test.} Six policies compete on identical traces. \emph{LRU} evicts the least-recently-accessed entry. A \emph{recency--frequency heuristic} scores each entry $0.4f + 0.4r + 0.2v$, where $f$ is its access count normalized over the candidate set, $r = \exp(-\ln 2 \cdot \Delta t / 30\text{min})$ decays with idle time $\Delta t$, and $v$ is its normalized token count; the lowest score is evicted. \emph{Logistic V1} evicts by lowest predicted $P(\text{resume})$ (\S\ref{sec:v1}). \emph{Value Density V2} and its admission-controlled variant \emph{V2+AC} evict by lowest value per byte, and \emph{Space-Time V3} by lowest lifetime-normalized value density (\S\ref{sec:std}). LRU and the heuristic consume only access order and counts; the remaining policies consume session features or learned predictions, which is exactly the dependence that \S\ref{sec:lru_strong} shows makes them fragile.

\textbf{Workloads.} We evaluated eviction policies using a synthetic workload simulator with three profiles via Poisson arrivals and Log-normal token distributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Casual:} 50/hr arrival, $\mu{=}512$, $\sigma{=}0.8$, low return probability.
  \item \textbf{Enterprise:} 200/hr, $\mu{=}2048$, $\sigma{=}0.5$, high return probability.
  \item \textbf{Power User:} 80/hr, $\mu{=}8192$, $\sigma{=}1.2$, medium return probability.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{User Personas.} Each simulated user carries a persistent persona (a return-propensity multiplier, a verbosity scale, and a 10-hour diurnal activity window) drawn once and reused across all of that user's sessions. Without personas, per-user features such as historical return rate are i.i.d.\ noise by construction and no predictor can exceed chance (we measured AUC~0.56 under an i.i.d.\ pool vs.\ 0.70 with personas). Conversations grow monotonically: each resume appends new turns to the previously cached context.

\textbf{Virtual Clock.} Traces are replayed on a simulated clock advanced to each event's timestamp. All components (the cache manager, eviction policies, and feature extraction) read time exclusively from this clock. This is essential for validity: replaying a 6-hour trace at wall-clock speed collapses every temporal feature (session age, idle time, hour of day) to near zero. That silently invalidates any policy that reasons about time, while order-based policies like LRU are unaffected.

\textbf{Model Configuration.} KV cache sizes are computed via Eq.~\ref{eq:kvsize} with a downscaled configuration (2~layers, 2~heads, head dimension~32, FP16; 512~bytes/token). Llama-2-7B geometry (\S2.1) is exactly $1{,}024\times$ larger per token; equivalently, our 500\,MB capacity stresses the same session-count-to-capacity ratio as a ${\sim}500$\,GB tier would for 7B-scale caches. Scaling all entries by a constant shifts absolute capacity dynamics but not relative policy comparisons.

\textbf{Simulation.} 6-hour continuous window; token counts clamped to 8,192 to preserve heavy-tailed distributions.

\textbf{Capacity.} 500MB total (50MB hot + 150MB warm + 300MB cold) in the main study; \S\ref{sec:regimes} sweeps 125MB--2GB at the same 10/30/60 split.

\textbf{GPU Savings.} Each hit is credited with the prefill it avoids for the tokens \emph{actually cached} (not the grown context at resume time), minus the modeled cost of restoring the entry from the tier where it was found. Both sides are priced from one architecture using the same expressions as the break-even model of \S\ref{sec:breakeven}: prefill costs $(2PN + 4LH_qdN^2)/(F\eta)$ and restore moves $2LH_{kv}db N$ bytes at the tier's latency and bandwidth. Pricing both sides together is what makes the accounting coherent: a hit whose restore exceeds the prefill it avoids is credited zero rather than counted as a win, so the value model agrees with \S\ref{sec:breakeven} about which hits are worth having.

We report value as \textbf{GPU-seconds saved per day}. Dollars are a linear rescaling ($\$2.50$/hr for an on-demand A100~80GB) that we quote where it aids interpretation, but the GPU-second is the scale-free quantity: it does not depend on the reader's negotiated GPU price, and at our workload's modest arrival rates the dollar figures say more about deployment size than about policies.

\textbf{Architecture is an experimental variable, not a constant.} Whether a hit is worth anything depends on the model being served, because prefill scales superlinearly in $N$ while restore scales linearly. We therefore run the matrix under two architectures: \textbf{TinyLlama-1.1B} (22\,KB/token; the model our end-to-end validation measures, \S\ref{sec:tinyllama}) and \textbf{Llama-2-70B} (320\,KB/token, GQA; representative of models large enough that persistence is deployed in practice). We also report Llama-2-7B as a limiting case: its MHA cache is so large per token that \emph{no} session in our workload clears break-even, making persistence the wrong design there rather than a policy problem (\S\ref{sec:breakeven}).

\textbf{Statistics.} Every (policy, workload) cell is run over 10 independent seeds. All policies replay the identical trace per seed, so policy comparisons are \emph{paired}: we report per-seed deltas against LRU with 95\% t-based confidence intervals across seeds.

\subsection{Real-Trace Workload: Azure LLM Inference}
\label{sec:realtrace}

To test external validity beyond synthetic workloads, we additionally replay the Azure LLM inference trace~\cite{azurepublicdataset,stojkovic2025dynamollm}: 27.3M production conversation requests spanning one week of May 2024, each carrying an arrival timestamp and context/generated token counts. Two properties of the trace transfer into our replay verbatim: the \emph{arrival process} (real burstiness and diurnal/weekly load shape; timestamps keep their true UTC hour and weekday on the simulator's Monday-00:00 time axis) and \emph{per-request context sizes} (heavy-tailed: median 928, mean 1{,}632, p95 4{,}943 tokens).

What the trace cannot provide is session identity: for customer privacy it carries no conversation identifiers, and we find linkage is not reconstructable either. A greedy matcher that chains requests by context-token growth (a follow-up turn's context must contain the previous turn's context plus its response) links 87.8\% of requests, but it links an indistinguishable 87.8\% on a \emph{permutation control} in which token counts are shuffled across requests, which destroys any true chains. At production density (${\sim}60$~req/s over at most 8{,}000 distinct context lengths) growth-chaining is collision-dominated, a caution for any study tempted to reconstruct conversations from these public traces. We therefore impose return behavior using the same persona machinery as the synthetic workloads (enterprise return-window parameters), with resume-time context growth drawn empirically from the window's generated-token distribution. Differences between the synthetic-enterprise and real-trace results are thus attributable to the arrival process and size distribution, which are real; return behavior is held fixed and modeled.

We slice ten 6-hour windows across the week (covering weekday/weekend and peak/trough load) and thin each to 300 session starts per hour by taking every $k$-th request, preserving the arrival shape. The thinning rate is chosen for capacity pressure: real Azure requests are markedly smaller than synthetic enterprise sessions, so at the enterprise arrival rate (200/h) the 500\,MB cache is unconstrained (LRU ${\approx}98\%$ hit rate, no policy differentiation, as with the casual workload), while 300/h restores sustained eviction pressure comparable to the enterprise regime. Each window is one replicate: all six policies replay the identical event list, so deltas remain paired, with windows in the role of seeds. The learned policies reuse the predictor trained on synthetic personas, a zero-shot transfer test. The conversion is implemented in \texttt{benchmarks/azure\_trace\_loader.py}.

\subsection{LRU Is a Stronger Baseline Than Assumed}
\label{sec:lru_strong}

LRU wins the metric we set out to beat. Under virtual-clock replay with paired multi-seed statistics, it attains the highest hit rate on every constrained workload: 79.1\% [75.9, 82.4] on enterprise against Logistic~V1's 67.9\% [66.8, 69.0], and 96.0\% against 83.9\% on power-user. No learned or value-aware policy we built comes close. Our original hypothesis is falsified as decisively as the data permits, and we recommend LRU without reservation to anyone whose objective really is hit rate.

That recommendation is narrower than it sounds, because on the enterprise workload LRU simultaneously delivers the \emph{least} value of any policy in Table~\ref{tab:results} (\S\ref{sec:decouple}). The two statements are consistent, and their combination is this paper's subject.

\textbf{Two bugs, both flattering, both ours.} We arrived here by correcting our own pipeline twice, and the corrections moved the verdict in opposite directions. First, an early harness replayed traces at wall-clock speed and credited hits with unclamped resume-time token counts; it showed a learned policy beating LRU by \$68/day. That was an artifact: collapsing simulated time zeroed the temporal features the model was trained on, and the accounting credited a different quantity than the policies optimized. Fixing it reversed the result, and we long reported LRU as winning on both metrics.

Second, and only after the analysis in this paper was otherwise complete, we found that our cost model contained no architecture at all: it priced a prefill with two hardcoded constants whose linear/quadratic balance placed the crossover at ${\sim}1{,}300$ tokens, where real architectures cross between 12k and 53k (\S\ref{sec:setup}). That is not a rescaling, and the invariance argument we had relied on, that a constant factor cannot change rankings, did not apply: the error inflated an 8k-token session's worth relative to a 512-token one by $3\text{--}4.5\times$, exaggerating precisely the effect the study measures. Re-pricing from architecture (and validating the new model against measured hardware, \S\ref{sec:tinyllama}) inverted the value comparison while leaving hit rates untouched.

The pattern is worth naming, because it is not symmetric. A policy that consumes only access order is insensitive to broken time, broken features, \emph{and} broken value models; every richer policy is exposed to all three. Bugs in an evaluation harness therefore do not perturb rankings randomly. They flatter whichever side of the comparison depends on the broken component: our time bug flattered the learned policy, our cost-model bug flattered LRU. We report both, and note that the only reason we caught the second is that a reader asked why a 598-token prefill was modeled at 57.7 seconds when the hardware measured 37 milliseconds.

\subsection{Lifetime Normalization Helps Only Under High Size Variance}
\label{sec:whystatic}

Value density trades cardinality for value deliberately: V2 holds 58.2\% hit rate against LRU's 79.1\% on enterprise while delivering the most value of any policy we tested. Its retained set is smaller and worth more, which is the intended behavior of a packing objective and, under correct economics, the winning one.

The space-time correction (V3) divides that same density by $\mathbb{E}[\Delta t]$ to bound sojourn time. It earns its keep only where sessions vary widely in size: on power-user ($\sigma{=}1.2$) V3 beats V2 by $+4.2$ GPU-s/day (paired 95\% CI $[+2.3, +6.1]$) with $+2.6$ hit-rate points, while on enterprise ($\sigma{=}0.5$) the two are statistically indistinguishable ($-0.8$\,$[-3.6,+1.9]$). This is what the Little's Law argument of \S\ref{sec:std} should predict on reflection: unbounded sojourn hurts when a policy can hoard entries far larger than average, and heavy-tailed size distributions are what create that opportunity. Admission control (V2+AC) never helps and costs $4.8$--$7.6$ GPU-s/day, confirming that under sustained load the cache runs at near-full utilization regardless of filtering at the door.

\textbf{A retraction.} An earlier version of this paper reported that static value density \emph{collapsed}, worst hit rate of any policy, with V3 recovering \$94/day over it, and offered that as evidence for the Little's Law diagnosis. That result did not survive the cost-model correction of \S\ref{sec:lru_strong}. The superseded model overstated a large session's worth relative to a small one by $3\text{--}4.5\times$, which is precisely the signal V2 maximizes; V2 hoarded accordingly and starved its own cardinality. Priced correctly, the same policy hoards less and wins. This is the clearest instance of a pattern we return to in \S\ref{sec:lru_strong}: the collapse we had diagnosed as a property of the \emph{objective} was a property of our \emph{cost model}, and only a value-aware policy could have been misled by it.

\subsection{Hit Rate and Value Decouple}
\label{sec:decouple}

On the enterprise workload the two metrics do not merely diverge, they \emph{reverse}. LRU leads on hit rate by 11.2 points over Logistic~V1 and by 21.3 over Value~Density~V2, yet it delivers the least value in the table: V2 saves $1.99\times$ as much GPU time (paired $+36.4$ GPU-s/day $[+30.4, +42.4]$), V3 $1.97\times$, V1 $1.73\times$, and even the crude recency--frequency heuristic $1.65\times$. Every one of those deltas is significant. Tuning for hit rate on this workload selects the lowest-value policy in the table.

\textbf{Why the reversal happens.} Hit rate weights every hit identically; value does not, for two compounding reasons visible in our accounting. First, a \emph{threshold} effect: a hit is worth nothing at all unless the prefill it avoids exceeds the restore it costs, i.e.\ unless the session exceeds the break-even length $N^{*}$ of \S\ref{sec:breakeven}. Under TinyLlama economics $N^{*}$ is 1,561 tokens from NVMe and 25,901 from object storage, so \emph{every} cold-tier hit in our token range is worth exactly zero. LRU, which is blind to both size and tier, earns 778 of its 1,206 enterprise hits in the cold tier; they inflate its hit rate and contribute nothing. Second, a \emph{magnitude} effect: above $N^{*}$, savings grow superlinearly in $N$, so retaining one 8k-token session can outweigh a dozen 2k-token ones. Value-aware policies concede many cheap hits to keep a few expensive ones, which is exactly the trade hit rate cannot express.

This also predicts when the metrics agree, which we confirm in two independent ways. On \emph{power-user} (median 8,192 tokens, far above $N^{*}$), nearly every hit clears the threshold and the metrics reconverge: no policy beats LRU by more than $1.08\times$ and most differences are not significant (Table~\ref{tab:results}). And on \emph{Llama-2-70B} economics, where grouped-query attention puts $N^{*}$ at 12 tokens from NVMe so that essentially all hits are valuable, LRU's hit-rate advantage largely does translate into value (\S\ref{sec:arch}). The metrics coincide when cached items are near-uniform in value, and diverge as that uniformity breaks, That uniformity is the assumption hit rate encodes, and heavy-tailed LLM workloads violate it.

The right target for KV-cache eviction, in both optimization and evaluation, is expected cache value, not hit rate.

\subsection{Ground Truth: Prediction Is the Binding Constraint}
\label{sec:oracle}

The results so far leave an alternative explanation open: perhaps the learned policies lose not because their objectives are wrong but because their predictor is weak (test AUC 0.70). To separate the two, we replay the same traces with \emph{oracle} policies that read the trace's actual future, which is legitimate here because replay is open-loop: arrivals never depend on cache behavior. Three oracles span the objective space. \emph{Belady} evicts the entry whose next access is farthest away, the classical clairvoyant baseline. \emph{Oracle-V1} runs V1's exact objective with a perfect classifier: $P(\text{resume within 1h})$ is the ground-truth indicator, with ties broken toward sooner-returning entries. \emph{Oracle-V3} runs V3's objective with perfect inputs: the ground-truth return indicator and the exact time to next access in place of the EMA estimate, making it a value-weighted greedy Belady.

Second, the headroom above LRU is larger than its hit-rate deficit suggests. LRU trails the oracles by 20.9 hit-rate points on enterprise but by $5.70\times$ in value ($+173.0$ GPU-s/day $[+167.5, +178.6]$), and by 4.0 points but $2.55\times$ on power-user. The ratio is inflated relative to the point gap for the reason developed in \S\ref{sec:decouple}: a clairvoyant policy not only hits more often, it hits on sessions that are worth something and serves them from tiers where the restore is cheap, whereas much of LRU's hit rate is spent on cold-tier entries worth zero.

Third, hit rate cannot distinguish the two oracles, yet their delivered value differs significantly. B\'{e}l\'{a}dy and Oracle~V3 both cache perfectly, 100.0\% versus 100.0\%, a paired difference of exactly zero on every seed, but Oracle~V3 delivers $+2.9$ GPU-s/day more on enterprise $[+2.0, +3.8]$ and $0.5$ less on power-user $[-0.8, -0.3]$, both significant. Once no session need ever be dropped, the only remaining decision is \emph{which tier} holds each entry, and hit rate is by construction blind to it: B\'{e}l\'{a}dy orders entries by next access time, while Oracle~V3 weights that ordering by recompute value and so keeps expensive sessions in fast tiers. Prediction error, capacity pressure, and policy heuristics are all absent here: two clairvoyant policies with identical hit rates deliver measurably different value.

These differences are small in absolute terms ($1.4\%$ and $0.4\%$). We do not claim tier placement is a large effect, only that it is a measurable one that hit rate reports as zero.

\subsection{How Much Does the Verdict Depend on Synthetic Predictability?}
\label{sec:persona}

Our resumption signal is generated, not observed. Each simulated user carries a
persona whose return propensity is drawn log-normally with dispersion $\sigma$, and
that dispersion sets a ceiling on how much per-user structure any predictor can
recover. The AUC of 0.70 reported in \S\ref{sec:v1} is therefore a property of our
generator as much as of our model, and a reader is entitled to ask whether the
conclusions are an artifact of choosing $\sigma$ favorably. We test this directly by
sweeping $\sigma$, retraining the predictor at each setting so the model is as good as
that world permits, and re-running the enterprise matrix.

Table~\ref{tab:persona} separates two claims that the main study confounds. The
\emph{learned policy's} standing does depend on $\sigma$, as it must: at $\sigma{=}0$,
where users are behaviorally identical and per-user features carry no information,
Logistic~V1 falls to $0.90\times$ LRU's value and the difference is not significant.
A classifier whose premise is per-user prediction should fail when there is no
per-user structure to predict, and it does.

The \emph{metric} finding does not depend on $\sigma$. Space-Time~V3 beats LRU on
value at every setting, from $1.34\times$ $[+5.9, +14.9]$ GPU-s/day at $\sigma{=}0$ to
$2.25\times$ $[+42.1, +60.0]$ at $\sigma{=}1.0$, and LRU leads on hit rate at every
setting except the last. This is what the mechanism of \S\ref{sec:decouple} predicts:
the threshold and magnitude effects come from the economics of prefill and restore,
not from how predictable the workload is, so removing all per-user signal cannot
remove them. Notably the gap is smallest exactly where LRU is strongest
($\sigma{=}0$, 95.3\% hit rate), which is the regime least favorable to our argument
and still not favorable enough to overturn it.

The sweep also sharpens a control reported in \S\ref{sec:setup}. Setting $\sigma{=}0$
does not drive AUC to chance but to 0.659, because recency, revisit count, and
time-of-day remain informative even when users are interchangeable. The earlier
i.i.d.-pool control, which reached AUC 0.56, destroyed user identity entirely and so
removed more than per-user dispersion alone. The two measure different things, and
$\sigma$ is the more precise instrument: it isolates the per-user component while
holding the rest of the feature set intact.

We do not claim this substitutes for observed return behavior. It bounds the
sensitivity of our conclusions to one modeling choice, and shows that the conclusion
which depends on that choice (whether learned eviction is worth building) is
separable from the one that does not (whether hit rate should be the target).

\section{Prototype Validation: TinyLlama}
\label{sec:tinyllama}

To validate with a real transformer, we integrated our \texttt{TieredCacheManager} with TinyLlama-1.1B-Chat~(22~layers, 32~Q-heads, 4~KV-heads via GQA, head\_dim=64) on CPU.

\textbf{GQA Compatibility.} Modern models use Grouped Query Attention where KV heads (4) differ from query heads (32). Our serialization must use \texttt{num\_key\_value\_heads} for tensor dimensions, a detail that is easy to get wrong in production.

\textbf{Metric definitions.} We report TTFT and end-to-end speedups \emph{separately}. All GPU timings are bracketed by \texttt{cuda.synchronize()}.

This is what the break-even model (\S\ref{sec:breakeven}) predicts: 598 tokens sits \emph{below} TinyLlama's crossover $N^{*}{\approx}1{,}561$, where persistence should not pay on latency. \S\ref{sec:breakeven} sweeps context length across the crossover.

\textbf{Determinism: storage is exactly as faithful as memory.} At 598 tokens every trial is byte-identical to the cold path on both devices. On short prompts (168 tokens), the resumed path deterministically diverges from the cold path at one near-tie token (both outputs coherent). An ablation attributes the cause: resuming from the \emph{never-serialized, in-memory} cache reproduces the restored-cache output exactly, in every trial, so the storage round-trip (including FP16 quantization under FP32 serving) contributes \emph{nothing}. The divergence stems entirely from computing the resumed token through incremental-decode kernels whose accumulation order differs from the cold path's batched prefill, which can flip near-tie argmax decisions. This is inherent to \emph{any} cache-reuse scheme, including in-memory prefix caching in production servers; tiered persistence adds no divergence beyond it.

\textbf{Cost model validation.} These measurements are also how we validate the value model that \S\ref{sec:eval} depends on, and how we caught its predecessor. The cost model used throughout our earlier evaluation predicted \textbf{57,747ms} for this 598-token prefill against ${\sim}$37ms measured on the T4, an error of roughly three orders of magnitude, because it priced prefill with two hardcoded constants and no model geometry at all. The architecture-parameterized model of \S\ref{sec:setup} predicts \textbf{47--71ms} for the same prefill at T4 specifications (peak 65 TFLOP/s, MFU 0.30--0.45), bracketing the measurement within a factor of two. Independently, its break-even lengths agree exactly with Table~\ref{tab:breakeven}, which is computed from the same expressions in separate code. We regard agreement within $2\times$ against hardware, plus internal consistency with the analytic model, as the minimum bar for a value model that policy conclusions rest on; the superseded model met neither, and no test in our suite would have caught it, because none existed.

\section{A Break-Even Model for Restoration versus Recomputation}
\label{sec:breakeven}

The 19.1$\times$ CPU TTFT speedup above evaporated to parity on a T4, where prefilling 598 tokens takes ${\sim}$37ms, comparable to the restore path itself. Persistence wins on raw latency only beyond a \emph{break-even context length} $N^{*}$, which we derive analytically below; the GPU measurements land where the model predicts.

Restoring a persisted cache moves $O(N)$ bytes:
\begin{equation}
  t_{\text{restore}}(N) = \ell_{\text{tier}} + \frac{2 \cdot L \cdot H_{kv} \cdot d \cdot b \cdot N}{B_{\text{tier}}}
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ is tier latency, $B$ bandwidth, $L$ layers, $H_{kv}$ KV heads, $d$ head dimension, $b$ bytes per element. Recomputing the prefill costs $O(N)$ weight FLOPs plus $O(N^2)$ attention:
\begin{equation}
  t_{\text{prefill}}(N) = \frac{2 P N + 4 L H_{q} d N^{2}}{F \cdot \eta}
\end{equation}
with $P$ parameters, $H_q$ \emph{query} heads (GQA shrinks the cache, not the score computation), $F$ peak FLOP/s, and $\eta$ the achievable utilization. Since restore is linear and prefill superlinear, persistence always wins beyond the crossover $N^{*}$.

\section{Discussion \& Future Work}

\textbf{The Metric Mismatch.} Hit rate assumes uniform item value. KV caching violates that assumption twice over: a hit below $N^{*}$ is worth literally zero, and above it savings grow superlinearly. The consequence is not a loose correlation but a reversal, with the hit-rate winner delivering the least value on our enterprise workload. Research and production systems should report value-weighted metrics (GPU-seconds saved per decision, discounted by restore cost); hit rate remains meaningful only after showing that cached items are near-uniform in value, which is a property of the workload and the served architecture, not something a policy can assume.

\textbf{Why does LRU win on hit rate, and lose on value?} It wins the first because our workloads have strong temporal locality: most resumes occur within an hour of the previous access, which is exactly LRU's inductive bias, obtained without training anything. It loses the second because the same blindness that makes it robust also makes it indiscriminate. Consuming only access order, LRU cannot tell a 512-token session from an 8k one, nor a hot-tier hit from a cold-tier hit that costs more to restore than to recompute; on enterprise traffic it earns 778 of its 1,206 hits in a tier where every hit is worth zero. Robustness and precision are the same property viewed from two sides, and which one matters depends entirely on whether hits are uniform in value.

\textbf{A caution on baselines.} LRU's insensitivity has a methodological consequence. It consumes one input, access order, while learned policies consume nine features whose scales must match training, and value-aware policies additionally consume a cost model. A defect in any of those inputs degrades the richer policy and leaves LRU untouched, so evaluation errors do not perturb rankings symmetrically. Both defects we found in our own pipeline behaved this way (\S\ref{sec:lru_strong}). We suspect published comparisons against LRU that do not control replay-time semantics and cost-model fidelity deserve re-examination in both directions: wins for learned eviction may be artifacts, and so may losses.

\textbf{Decision Framework.} (1)~Compute $N^{*}$ for the served architecture and tier first (\S\ref{sec:breakeven}); it determines everything that follows. (2)~If the workload's context lengths sit far above $N^{*}$, as on large GQA models where $N^{*}$ is tens of tokens, hits are near-uniform in value, hit rate is an adequate proxy, and LRU is the right choice: it is free, robust, and hard to beat. (3)~If the distribution straddles $N^{*}$, hit rate is actively misleading and value-aware eviction pays; a static value-density objective suffices, and lifetime normalization adds significance only under high size variance ($\sigma{=}1.2$). (4)~If context lengths sit largely below $N^{*}$, as for MHA models from object storage, no policy helps because persistence itself is value-negative; use fewer tiers rather than a better policy. (5)~Never tune on hit rate without first establishing which of these regimes applies.

\textbf{Limitations.} (1)~The real-trace evaluation replays production arrivals and sizes, but session \emph{return behavior} remains modeled. \S\ref{sec:persona} bounds how much this matters by sweeping the parameter that controls it, and finds the metric conclusion stable across the range, but a bound is not a measurement: public LLM inference traces carry no conversation identifiers, and we show linkage is not reconstructable from them (\S\ref{sec:realtrace}). Datasets with hashed conversation identifiers (e.g., WildChat, LMSYS-Chat-1M) could close this gap. (2)~GPU validation used a single T4, and the context sweep crosses $N^{*}$ only barely (1,792 tokens vs.\ $N^{*}{\approx}1{,}561$); the region well above the crossover, and server-class GPUs (A100/H100, where higher FLOPs push $N^{*}$ further out), remain unmeasured. (3)~Tier restore costs are modeled, not measured on production hardware. (4)~The simulation stores a downscaled KV geometry as a scale model: capacity dynamics run on 512-byte-per-token entries while value and restore are priced at the served architecture, so absolute occupancy is not physical even though the session-count-to-capacity ratio and the economics are. (5)~Value is modeled throughout, from measured hardware constants but not from an instrumented production server; the absolute GPU-seconds should be read as the output of a validated model (\S\ref{sec:tinyllama}) rather than as a measurement.

\textbf{What remains open.} Value-aware eviction already beats LRU on value where the workload straddles $N^{*}$, but no policy we built wins \emph{both} metrics anywhere, and the most promising design would not have to: a hybrid that follows recency by default and deviates only for entries in the value tail should capture LRU's locality while avoiding its indiscriminate cold-tier hits. Two further directions follow from our results. Tier-aware admission, refusing to promote entries whose restore cost exceeds their recompute value, computable in closed form from $N^{*}$, should recover most of LRU's wasted hits at no prediction cost. And because our value model makes cold-tier hits worthless for small models, the tier hierarchy itself should be architecture-dependent rather than fixed.

\section{Conclusion}

We set out to beat LRU at cache eviction with a learned policy, and failed: across three synthetic workloads, ten paired seeds, two model architectures, and a replay of the Azure LLM inference production trace, LRU wins cache hit rate every time.

That verdict, however, does not mean what it appears to mean. Measured in GPU time actually saved, the same LRU that wins every hit-rate comparison delivers the least value of any policy on our enterprise workload, where a value-density policy conceding 21 hit-rate points saves 1.99$\times$ as much compute. Hit rate treats all hits as equal; KV caching makes them unequal twice over, since a hit below the break-even length $N^{*}$ is worth nothing at all and savings above it grow superlinearly with context. Where those effects are absent, on workloads far above $N^{*}$, or on large grouped-query models where $N^{*}$ falls to tens of tokens: the metrics agree and LRU is genuinely the right choice. Where the workload straddles $N^{*}$, optimizing hit rate selects the worst policy available and reports it as the best.

The clearest evidence is an ablation in which everything else is removed: two clairvoyant policies, both caching perfectly at a 100.0\% hit rate, still differ significantly in delivered value, because the tier an entry sits in determines what its hit is worth and hit rate cannot represent that. We arrived here by correcting our own evaluation twice, and the corrections moved in opposite directions: one flattered the learned policy, the other flattered LRU and had supported a collapse-of-value-density finding that did not survive re-pricing. Because a recency policy consumes strictly less information than its competitors, it is strictly less exposed to defects in that information, and evaluation errors consequently favor whichever side depends on the broken part. We think that asymmetry, more than any individual number in this paper, is what the learned-caching literature should take from it.

Beating LRU on both metrics at once remains open. Our results suggest the path is not a better predictor but a cheaper form of discrimination: tier-aware admission that refuses hits worth less than their restore, computable in closed form from $N^{*}$, with recency doing the work everywhere else.
